## Summary
British administration in Assam commenced with David Scott's appointment in 1823, leading to a series of treaties and annexations of various kingdoms like Jaintia, Cachar, and Lower Assam by 1828. David Scott implemented significant military, revenue, and judicial reforms, but British rule also sparked early revolts by figures like Gomdhar Konwar and the Khasis. Despite a brief restoration of the Ahom monarchy under Purandar Singha via the Treaty of Guwahati in 1833, the British eventually annexed Upper Assam, Sadiya, Muttock, Cachar, and Jayantia by 1839-1854, driven by economic interests and administrative challenges.

## Key Facts
- David Scott was appointed agent to the Governor General for the northeastern region in 1823.
- The Treaty with Ram Singh (Raja of Jaintia) and the Treaty of Badarpur (with Govinda Chandra, Raja of Cachar) established British vassalage in 1824.
- Khamtis, Moamarias, and Singpho chiefs accepted British suzerainty by 1826.
- Lower Assam up to Biswanath was annexed by the British in 1828, and Assam was made a non-regulated province.
- David Scott introduced the 'Assam Light Infantry' and implemented revenue reforms, including a Rs. 3 tax on paiks in Upper Assam and a professional tax in Lower Assam.
- Land survey was completed under Cpt. Mathews in Lower Assam, and tax-free lands (lakhiraj lands) were brought under revenue collection.
- Judicial reforms included civil cases tried by revenue heads in Upper Assam and the senior commissioner in Lower Assam, with moffusil panchayats to reduce court congestion.
- The First Revolt in 1828 was led by Gomdhar Konwar and Dhanjay Bargohain, with Gomdhar sentenced to seven years imprisonment.
- Peali Phukan and Jeoram Dulia Barua were hanged in Sibsagar in 1830 during a second revolt attempt.
- The Khasis, led by Teerut Singh, revolted in 1829 against a road construction project, killing British officers.
- The Treaty of Guwahati was signed on March 2, 1833, restoring a portion of Upper Assam to Purandar Singha, who agreed to pay Rs. 50,000 annually as tribute.
- Purandar Singha's territory was annexed in 1838 due to complaints, discovery of tea, coal, and petroleum, and an unsatisfactory report by Captain F. Jenkins.
- Sadiya was annexed after the Khamtis rebelled in 1839, killing political agent Adam White.
- Muttock was annexed in December 1839, taking advantage of internal strife.
- Cachar was annexed in 1832 after the assassination of Govinda Chandra, and North Cachar in 1854 after Tularam's death.
- Jayantia was annexed in 1835 after Rajendra Singh succeeded Ram Singh.

## Memory Hooks
- Scott's Sequence: David Scott's involvement spans his appointment (1823), early annexations (1828), administrative reforms, and the proposal for Ahom restoration before his death (1831).
- Revolts & Restorations: Early revolts (Gomdhar, Peali, Khasis) led to the idea of Ahom restoration, but this was short-lived, followed by more annexations.
- ABC of Annexations: A chronological sequence of territories falling under British control: Assam (Lower), Badarpur (Cachar), Cachar, Jayantia, Muttock, Sadiya, Upper Assam.

## Quick Revision
- British administration in Assam began with David Scott in 1823, leading to early annexations and treaties.
- David Scott implemented comprehensive military, revenue, and judicial reforms across Upper and Lower Assam.
- Early revolts by Gomdhar Konwar, Peali Phukan, and the Khasis challenged British authority.
- The Treaty of Guwahati (1833) briefly restored Ahom monarchy under Purandar Singha in Upper Assam.
- Driven by economic interests (tea, coal, petroleum) and administrative issues, the British annexed Upper Assam (1838), Sadiya (1839), Muttock (1839), Cachar (1832/1854), and Jayantia (1835).
